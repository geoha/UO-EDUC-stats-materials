Chapter 8 Classical Test Theory

8.1 True score model

Classical test theory (CTT) provides the rationale for many of the methods of estimating reliability. The fundamental premise is that a person’s observed score, \(X\), on a test (or test item) is explained by two things: their true status on the operationalized construct, \(T\) , and random error, \(E\). \[X = T + E\] This is the true score model.

The following hypothetical data set, which we’ll call Test A, shows this relationship for each person. For example, Alba’s true score is 12.26. The random error that was present in her observed score was -0.81. These sum to 11.45.
Table 8.1: Hypothetical data, Test A
PID T E X
Alba 12.26 -0.81 11.45
Bee 15.59 2.19 17.78
Carly 20.94 2.36 23.30
Dahlia 17.53 -0.82 16.71
Elly 15.70 -1.90 13.80
Frida 15.79 -2.09 13.70
Greta 18.29 -1.44 16.85
Hildre 11.91 2.50 14.42

We can never know a person’s true score, so we can never know how much of their observed score is indeed made up of this randomness. What we can do, though, if we have multiple items (or sub-tests), is estimate this proportion given the patterns of responses across multiple observations. We’ll practice methods of estimating reliability in the next chapter.

Not surprisingly, because \(X = T + E\), the same pattern occurs in expectation, \(\text{E}(X) = \text{E}(T) + \text{E}(E)\). We see the means in our hypothetical data representing this pattern:

T E X
Mean 16.00 0.00 16.00

Also, notice that the mean of the error is \(0\). This is because error is assumed to be random.

Take a look at this next fake data set, Test B. Notice that each person’s true score is the same as it was in Test A but that the errors and therefore the observed scores are different from those in Test A.

In Test A, Bee’s observed score is 17.78, which is higher than her true score, 15.59. In Test B, her observed score, 12.12, is lower than her true score. It may be that, for reasons beyond those related to construct being measured, Bee was feeling good during one test but not so good during the other test and her mood had an effect on her motivation.

Other participants’ variations away from their true scores are also assumed to be due to conditions unrelated to the construct being measured by the test—things such as their physical comfort or their random lapses in attention. These sources of error are outside of our control (usually). Another source of random error can be due to features in the test tasks, such as item wording, that result in idiosyncratic responses by the participants. In a test with high reliability, these discrepancies are relatively small in magnitude and the proportion of variance in the observed scores that is explained by error is low.

Looking at the magnitude of the errors, do you think Test B is better than, worse than, or about the same as Test A in terms of how much of the observed scores’ variability is due to error? It is difficult to tell by eyeballing the errors. Let’s look at the variance of the error in each of two hypothetical tests:

T E X
Test A 7.88 3.50 11.38
Test B 7.88 5.25 13.14

With these hypothetical data, the variance of the true scores is the same for the two tests, but they differ in how much error variance contributes to the scores. The test with less error is more reliable at estimating persons’ true scores from their observed scores. That is, with Test A, we have more confidence that—in general across all of the examinees—the observed scores provide a better representation of the examinees’ true scores than they do in Test B.

We are able to observe this with our hypothetical data because we have omniscient access to the true scores and error scores. In reality, we do not have this knowledge. We only have the observed scores. To estimate reliability, we examine the patterns in the observed data and rely on a set of assumptions of CTT.

8.2 Assumptions of the CTT true score model

We’ve seen the first assumption of the true score model. There are five more:

\(\begin{aligned} & 1.\;\; X = T + E\\[3pt] & 2.\;\; T = \text{E}(X)\\[3pt] & 3.\;\; \text{E}(E) = 0\\[3pt] & 4.\;\; \rho_{ET} = 0\\[3pt] & 5.\;\; \rho_{E_{i}E_{j}} = 0\\[3pt] & 6.\;\; \rho_{E_{i}T_{j}} = 0 \end{aligned}\)

The second assumption is that a person’s true score is the expectation of that person’s observed score over a multitude of occasions of taking that test. These multiple conditions are not realistically feasible because after each test we would have to wipe the person’s mind and send them back in time to take the test again. What we can do is generalize this assumption across a population of respondents: The expected observed score in the population is assumed to be the true score in the population.

The third assumption states that the expected error—across a multitude of occasions of observations with that person—will be zero. This also applies across multiple, statistically independent58, respondents. This makes sense because error is random, so in expectation it should have a mean of zero.

The fourth assumption is that the correlation between the true scores and the error is zero. This again is consistent with the notion that error is random. In expectation, error should have a correlation of zero with anything else in the test besides the observed scores (remember that the observed scores are made up of that error, \(X = T + E\), so they will correlate).

The fifth and sixth assumptions are similar to the fourth. The error on one test, \(E_i\), is unrelated to the error on another test, \(E_j\), and it is unrelated to the true scores on another test measuring the same thing, \(T_j\).

These assumptions underlie the logic of the reliability estimation procedures. They are in expectation—that is, they are what is assumed to be true with many independent observations of the test condition, such as on repeated occasions with persons (who are memory-wiped and sent back in time) or across a large population of persons. With real data sets, we will never know persons’ true scores or error scores. Even if we could, we would not likely observe these patterns with a single sample of test data because expectation concerns the expected value in the population.

8.3 Reliability and the variance of the composite score

In the true score model, the observed score, \(X\), is a composite of \(T\) and \(E\). Given that, we can assume that \(\text{Var}(X) = \text{Var}(T) + \text{Var}(E) + 2\text{Cov}(T,E)\). However, based on Assumption 4, in which \(\rho_{ET} = 0\), the covariance between \(T\) and \(E\) must be zero, \(\text{Cov}(T,E) = 0\). Stated another way, error is assumed to be random and therefore independent of the true scores. With this, under CTT, we therefore accept that \(\sigma^2_X = \sigma^2_T + \sigma^2_E\).

One of the clearest accounts of reliability is from Furr (2018). We can follow his description of four ways to think about reliability: as the ratio of true-score to observed-score variance, as the lack of error variance, as the correlation between observed and true scores, and as the correlation between two tests that are assumed to fit a parallel model.

8.3.1 Reliability as the ratio of true-score variance to observed-score variance

If there is any error, and there likely always is with measurement in education and the social sciences, the variance of the observed scores will always be larger than the variance of the true scores. When we report reliability in our work, we are interested in what proportion of the observed (total) variance is explained by the true score variance: \[\text{Rel} = \frac{\sigma^2_T}{\sigma^2_X}\] This true-score to observed-score variance proportion is one definition of reliability.59

8.3.2 Reliability as the lack of error variance

We can also think of reliability as the lack of error variance in the total observed variance. This is because \(\sigma^2_T = \sigma^2_X - \sigma^2_E\). \[\begin{eqnarray*} \text{Rel} &=& \frac{\sigma^2_X - \sigma^2_E}{\sigma^2_X}\\ &=& \frac{\sigma^2_X}{\sigma^2_X} - \frac{\sigma^2_E}{\sigma^2_X}\\ &=& 1 - \frac{\sigma^2_E}{\sigma^2_X} \end{eqnarray*}\]

8.3.3 Reliability as the squared correlation between observed and true scores

The reliability coefficient can also be understood as the squared correlation between the true and observed scores. Let’s show that this is equivalent to our earlier definition regarding the ratio of true-score to observed-score variance:60 \[\begin{eqnarray} \rho_{X,T}^2 &=& \left[\frac{\text{Cov}(X,T)}{S_{X}S_{T}}\right]^2 = \left[\frac{\sigma_{X,T}}{\sigma_{X}\sigma_{T}}\right]^2 = \frac{\sigma_{T}^2}{\sigma_{X}^2} \end{eqnarray}\] Let’s break this down by seeing how we get from \(\left[\frac{\sigma_{X,T}}{\sigma_{X}\sigma_{T}}\right]^2\) to \(\frac{\sigma_{T}^2}{\sigma_{X}^2}\).

For starters, let’s demonstrate that the numerator in the correlation coefficient (the covariance between \(X\) and \(T\)) is the same as the variance of the true scores. This makes sense because the error in \(X\) should not contribute to the correlation, so the systematic variance that is left in \(X\) is actually the variance of \(T\). In the following equations, we rely on Assumptions 1 and 3 of the CTT true score model. \[\begin{eqnarray*} \text{Cov}(X,T) &=& \text{E}\big((X-\bar{X})(T-\bar{T})\big)\\ &=& \text{E}\Big(\big(T + E -(\bar{T} + \bar{E})\big)\big(T-\bar{T}\big)\Big)\\ &=& \text{E}\big((T + E -\bar{T})(T-\bar{T})\big)\\ &=& \text{E}(T^2 -T\bar{T} + ET - E\bar{T} - T\bar{T} + \bar{T}^2)\\ &=& \text{E}(T^2) -\bar{T}\text{E}(T) + \text{E}(E)\text{E}(T) - \bar{T}\text{E}(E) - \bar{T}\text{E}(T) + \bar{T}^2\\ &=& \text{E}(T^2) -\bar{T}^2 + 0 - 0 - \bar{T}^2 + \bar{T}^2\\ &=& \text{E}(T^2) -\bar{T}^2\\ &=& \text{E}(T^2) -\big(\text{E}(T)\big)^2\\ \text{Cov}(X,T) &=& \text{Var}(T) \end{eqnarray*}\] Next, let’s plug that into the numerator of our correlation and simplify it: \[\begin{eqnarray*} \rho_{X,T} = \frac{\sigma_{X,T}}{\sigma_{X}\sigma_{T}} &=& \frac{\sigma_{T}^2}{\sigma_{X}\sigma_{T}}\\[5 pt] &=& \frac{\sigma_{T}}{\sigma_{X}} \end{eqnarray*}\] Let’s now square that so the denominator is the total variance: \[\begin{eqnarray*} \rho_{X,T}^2 &=& \frac{\sigma_{T}^2}{\sigma_{X}^2} \end{eqnarray*}\] And Bob’s your uncle.

8.3.4 Reliability as the correlation between scores on parallel tests

With two parallel tests, we can estimate the reliability as their correlation. Let’s show that this is equivalent to our earlier definition regarding the ratio of true-score to observed-score variance. \[\begin{eqnarray} \rho_{X_1,X_2} &=& \left[\frac{\text{Cov}(X_1,X_2)}{S_{X_1}S_{X_2}}\right] = \left[\frac{\sigma_{X_1,X_2}}{\sigma_{X_1}\sigma_{X_2}}\right] = \frac{\sigma_{T}^2}{\sigma_{X}^2} \end{eqnarray}\]

Because two parallel tests have equal variances (and therefore equal standard deviations), the denominator, \(\sigma_{X_1}\sigma_{X_2}\), is the variance of either test, \(\sigma_{X_1}^2\) or \(\sigma_{X_2}^2\), or simply \(\sigma_{X}^2\) .

The covariance of their components is \[\begin{eqnarray} \text{Cov}(X_1,X_2) &=& \text{Cov}(T_1,T_2) + \text{Cov}(T_1,E_2) + \text{Cov}(E_1,T_2) + \text{Cov}(E_1,E_2)\\[5pt] &=& \text{Cov}(T_1,T_2) + 0 + 0 + 0 \end{eqnarray}\]

Because \(T_1\) = \(T_2\), and the covariance of a variable with itself is variance, \[\begin{eqnarray} \text{Cov}(X_1,X_2) &=& \text{Cov}(T_1,T_2) = \text{Cov}(T_1,T_1)\\[5pt] &=& \sigma_T^2 \end{eqnarray}\] Plugging this into the numerator of \(\left[\frac{\sigma_{X_1,X_2}}{\sigma_{X_1}\sigma_{X_2}}\right]\), and replacing the denominator with \(\sigma_{X}^2\), \[\begin{eqnarray} \rho_{_{X_1,X_2}} &=& \frac{\sigma_{T}^2}{\sigma_{X}^2} \end{eqnarray}\]

This is the rationale for estimating the reliability coefficient in test-retest reliability or alternate-forms reliability as the correlation coefficient. It assumes a parallel model, which is described next.

8.4 Four CTT models

In most tests, there are several items that are intended to measure the same construct. These items can be thought of as smaller mini tests or sub-tests. Because we cannot observe the true scores on any given test, we rely on the shared variances, or covariances, among items. As we saw above, the covariance between an observed score and the true score is the same as the variance of the true scores. This is because the error washes out. The same principle applies to covariances among items—their shared variance, after we remove error, what we have is the true-score variance.

In each of the four hypothetical examples that follow to illustrate the four CTT models, we’ll be looking at only two tests (such as two items or two sub-tests). When we have many items (which again we’re calling tests or sub-tests here), the same assumptions about the relationships among those items would apply. Also, the tests don’t have to be items or mini tests or sub-tests. With test-retest reliability methods and alternative-form methods, we have two full tests and these same assumptions apply. With this, we use the generic term tests in this section but what we’re talking about can be applied to items, sub-tests, or alternate test forms.

There are several methods for estimating reliability (such as test-retest reliability, coefficient alpha, and coefficient omega). In addition to the general assumptions of CTT, each method has additional assumptions having to do with the linear relationships among the true scores on these mini tests. These assumptions are classifiable into four CTT models:

  1. Parallel,
  2. Tau-equivalent,
  3. Essentially tau-equivalent, and
  4. Congeneric.

8.4.1 The parallel model

The parallel model is the most stringent. It assumes that the true score of each person is the same on both tests and that both tests are of the same difficulty. This linear relationship between the true scores on one test and those on the other is represented using regression: \(T_2 = a + bT_1\), with \(a = 0\) and \(b = 1\). The intercept represents the test difficulty. If \(a \neq 0\), the true scores would be lower on the more difficult test. In the parallel model, the two tests are assumed to be equally difficult. The parallel test model also assumes that the error variances on both tests are the same, \(\sigma^2_{E_1} = \sigma^2_{E_2}\). Here are hypothetical data from two tests that are parallel:

8.4.1.1 If we cannot assume a parallel test model

If the two tests are not parallel, the error variances will probably differ (\(\sigma_{E_i}^2 \neq \sigma_{E_j}^2\)), which in turn will lead to unequal observed variances (and standard deviations) because \(\sigma_{X_i}^2 = \sigma_{T_i}^2 + \sigma_{E_i}^2\). Because of this, the denominator in the equation used above to show that reliability of either test is the correlation between scores on two parallel tests, \(\left[\frac{\sigma_{X_1,X_2}}{\sigma_{X_1}\sigma_{X_2}}\right]\), cannot be replaced with the variance of either test, \(\sigma_{X}^2\).

Another way to think about this is that if the two tests cannot be assumed to have the same error variances, the proportion of the total variance that is explained by the true score variance will also likely differ in each test: \[\begin{eqnarray} \text{Because it is likely that }\;\; {\sigma^2_{E_1}} &\neq& {\sigma^2_{E_2}} \text{, }\\[5pt] \text{and because } \;\; \sigma_{X_i}^2 &=& \sigma_{T_i}^2 + \sigma_{E_i}^2 \text{, }\\[5pt] \text{it is also likely that }\;\; \frac{\sigma^2_{E_1}}{\sigma^2_{X_1}} &\neq& \frac{\sigma^2_{E_2}}{\sigma^2_{X_2}}\text{. }\\[20pt] \text{Therefore, it is likely that }\;\; 1- \frac{\sigma^2_{E_1}}{\sigma^2_{X_1}} &\neq& 1- \frac{\sigma^2_{E_2}}{\sigma^2_{X_2}}\text{,}\\[5pt] \text{which is the same as saying that }\;\; \frac{\sigma^2_{T_1}}{\sigma^2_{X_1}} &\neq& \frac{\sigma^2_{T_2}}{\sigma^2_{X_2}} \text{ .}\\[5pt] \text{That is, it is likely that }\;\; \text{Rel}_{Test_1} &\neq& \text{Rel}_{Test_2} \text{ .} \end{eqnarray}\]

Even if we have a tau-equivalent or essentially-tau-equivalent model (described next), in which the true-score variances are equal (\(\sigma^2_{T_i} = \sigma^2_{T_j}\)), the reliabilities of the sub-tests will likely differ. Therefore, if we cannot assume a parallel model, the correlation between the observed scores cannot serve as a confident estimate of either of the two tests’ reliability estimates. Nonetheless, this procedure tends to be used in practice when practitioners use a correlation coefficient to estimate test-retest or alternate-forms reliability coefficients. When practitioners do this, they are relying on the assumption that the two tests have equal error variances (in addition to their having equal true-score means and variances).

8.4.2 The tau-equivalent model

The tau-equivalent model assumes the same linear relationship between tests’ true scores as does the parallel model, with \(T_2 = a + bT_1\), where \(a = 0\) and \(b = 1\). Unlike the parallel model, this model does not assume that the two tests’ error variance are equal. With this, the observed-score variances can differ between the tests. Our example tests A and B from the beginning of this chapter are tau equivalent. Here they are again, as Test 1 and 3.

8.4.3 The essentially tau-equivalent model

This model does not assume that the two tests are of equal difficulty. In other words, the intercepts in the linear relationship can differ. That is, the \(a\) parameter in \(T_2 = a + bT_1\) can differ from \(0\). When we use coefficient alpha, we are assuming that at least this model is present. It allows for the items to differ in difficulty but it assumes that their relationships with the construct, or true score, are the same across the items.

8.4.4 The congeneric model

This is the most flexible model and probably the most realistic. The true-score component is the same on both tests, but its relationship with each item or sub-test may be on a different scale and have different strength of relationships with the construct. So, in addition to \(a\) not having to equal \(0\), \(b\) does not have to equal \(1\). When we use coefficient omega, this model is acceptable. 8.5 Estimating an observation’s true score

We can never really know a person’s true score, but we can use regression to estimate it if we assume that \(T = \text{E}(X)\), and by implication that \(\text{E}(T) = \text{E}(X)\), or \(\bar{T} = \bar{X}\). In a simple regression, we can estimate \(\hat{Y}_{i}\) of person \(i\) from the covariance of the predictor and outcome variable, \(\sigma_{X,Y}\), the variance of the predictor, \(\sigma_X^2\), and the mean of the two variables, \(\bar{X}\) and \(\bar{Y}\):

\[\begin{eqnarray*} \hat{Y}_{i} &=& \frac{\sigma_{X,Y}}{\sigma_X^2}(X - \bar{X}) + \bar{Y} \\[5pt] \end{eqnarray*}\]

Here, we’re interested in predicting the true score, \(\hat{T}\), instead of \(\hat{Y}\). Unfortunately, we never know \(T\) or \(\bar{T}\). However, we can work around this because we can assume, from our CTT assumptions, that \(T = \text{E}(X)\) and therefore that \(\bar{T} = \bar{X}\). So, we can plug in the mean observed score, \(\bar{X}\), in place of the mean of the true score, \(\bar{T}\). Also, we know from above that the covariance between observed and true scores (\(\sigma_{X,T}\)) is, according to CTT, the same as \(\sigma_{T}^2\). So, to predict Person \(i\)’s true score, we can treat \(\frac{\sigma_{X,T}}{\sigma_X^2}\) as \(\frac{\sigma_{T}^2}{\sigma_X^2}\):

\[\begin{eqnarray*} \hat{T}_{i} &=& \frac{\sigma_{X,T}}{\sigma_X^2}(X_i - \bar{X}) + \bar{T} \\[5pt] \hat{T}_{i} &=& \frac{\sigma_{T}^2}{\sigma_X^2}(X_i - \bar{X}) + \bar{T} \\[5pt] \hat{T}_{i} &=& \text{Rel}(X_i - \bar{X}) + \bar{X} \\[5pt] \end{eqnarray*}\]

In this way, the true score is a linear transformation of the observed score, and a theoretical formulation based on classical test theory and regression.

Let’s say we administered a test intended for norm-referenced interpretations. The mean score was \(16\) and the reliability of the test scores was \(.69\). If Alba scored \(11.45\), what is our best estimate of her true score? We can create an R function using our formula:

Ts_predicter <- function(x, xbar, rel) {
       T_hat <- rel*(x - xbar) + xbar
       T_hat
}

Let’s try out our custom function, which we’ve named Ts_predicter:

Pred_ts_Alba <- Ts_predicter(x = 11.45, xbar = 16, rel = .69)
Pred_ts_Alba
## [1] 12.8605

This is the predicted true score of Alba. Again, we have no way of really knowing a person’s true score.

With a person’s observed score, along with the mean of the test and the reliability of the scores, we can predict a person’s true score. If all we know is a person’s observed score, that will be our best estimate of their true score.

8.6 The standard error of measurement

Obviously, if we wish to have a precise instrument, we seek to have a small amount of error in estimating examinees’ true scores. We can think of this at the test level as well as at the individual examinee level over repeated independent test administrations (with mind wipes and back-to-the test context time travel). The standard error of measurement, \(\sigma_E\), is the standard deviation of the errors in this theoretical repeated independent test administration.

As mentioned above, we can think of reliability as the lack of error variance, \(1 - \frac{\sigma^2_E}{\sigma^2_X}\), because of the direct relationship, \(\sigma^2_X = \sigma^2_T + \sigma^2_E\). From this, we can also see that \(\sigma_E^2\) is the unexplained portion of the observed variance. The relationship between reliability and \(\sigma_E\) can thought of in terms of variance:

\[\begin{eqnarray*} \text{Rel} &=& 1 - \frac{\sigma^2_E}{\sigma^2_X}\\[5pt] \therefore \frac{\sigma^2_E}{\sigma^2_X} &=& 1 - Rel \\[5pt] \sigma^2_E &=& (1-\text{Rel})\sigma_{X}^2 \end{eqnarray*}\]

In other words, the error variance is what is left over in the observed variance after we account for reliability. Variance is in squared units. If we seek to get to the standard error, we take the square root:

\[\begin{eqnarray*} \sigma_E &=& \sqrt{(1-\text{Rel})\sigma_{X}^2} \\[5pt] \sigma_E &=& \sigma_{X}\sqrt{(1-\text{Rel})} \\[5pt] \end{eqnarray*}\]

If an assessment document provides a report of the reliability and of the standard deviation (or variance) of the observed scores, we can calculate the estimated standard error.

The implication is this: If many (theoretical) random samples of this parallel test were administered to Person \(i\), 95% of them would have confidence intervals that include their true score.

8.6.1 Estimating the confidence interval of a true score

If we know the mean and variance of the observed scores on a test, as well as the reported reliability, we can estimate a confidence interval around an estimated true score. Let’s use Alba’s predicted true score.

CIer <- function(x, rel, VarX ) {
        Var_E  <- (1-rel)*VarX
        SE_Msr <- sqrt(Var_E) 
        LB     <- x - 1.96*SE_Msr 
        UB     <- x + 1.96*SE_Msr
   round(c(SE = SE_Msr, LowerBound = LB,Pred_Sc = x, UpperBound=UB),3)  }

Now, let’s try out our custom function with Alba’s predicted true score:

CIer(x = Pred_ts_Alba, rel = .69, VarX = 11.38)
##         SE LowerBound    Pred_Sc UpperBound 
##      1.878      9.179     12.860     16.542

Some researchers do not report the predicted true score and simply report the confidence interval of a particular observed score. They would use Alba’s observed score of \(11.45\) in this calculation:

CIer(x = 11.45, rel = .69, VarX = 11.38)
##         SE LowerBound    Pred_Sc UpperBound 
##      1.878      7.769     11.450     15.131

8.6.1.1 Using confidence intervals for making decisions about examinees

Estimating the confidence interval around a particular score—whether it be the predicted true score or the observed score—has implications for making decisions based on scores. If a person’s predicted true score (or perhaps their observed score) is near a particular cut score, their confidence interval may include that cut-off score. If we are making decisions about that person based on whether or not they scored at or above this cut score, we need to consider this uncertainty.

The stakes of wrong decisions should play a role in our choice of how large a confidence interval to estimate. If we seek to be very cautious, we can use a broader confidence interval, such as with statistical alpha set to .01 instead of .05. The \(1.96\) critical \(Z\) that we used in our formula was based on the 95% confidence interval (with alpha set to .05). With a 99% confidence interval, our critical \(Z\) will be \(2.58\), which we could insert into our formula in place of \(1.96\).61 This more cautious confidence interval, which would be appropriate for high-stakes decisions, will have a larger range thereby increasing our range of uncertainty. An example of a high-stakes decision would be whether we grant the examinee a license to perform surgery. With Alba’s estimated true score, this more conservative confidence interval would be:

Table 8.14: 99% confidence interval of Alba’s estimated true score
SE Lower bound Pred. TS Upper bound
1.88 8.02 12.86 17.70

Additionally, the direction of our caution should also depend on the nature of the decision. For instance, a cut score for determining whether students should receive further instructional help can err on the side of false positives. If the cut score for classification into this program were set as \(\leq 10\), we might admit Alba even though her score was above 10 because her 95% confidence interval (9.18, 16.54) included that value. Probably the best approach to making this decision would be to use this test score along with additional information such as from reports from her teachers about her performance or an interview with her. Doing this for all students might be costly, so we might restrict this additional data collection to students whose confidence intervals included the cut score.

In contrast, if our decision were a credential that involved high stakes, such as for licensure to become a surgeon, we should err on the side of false negatives. We should either set the cut score very high or ensure that the lower bound of the person’s confidence interval is above the cut-off criterion before licensing that person to perform surgery. If we used the 99% confidence interval and a cut score of \(\gt 10\), we would not yet recommend Alba for this license to perform surgery.62
