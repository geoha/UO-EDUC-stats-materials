Chapter 9 Internal-consistency Reliability

In this chapter, we begin by revealing the inner workings of coefficient alpha63, which is the most frequently used method for estimating the internal-consistency reliability coefficient. We will explore two packages for estimating alpha, the CTT package (Willse, 2018) and the psych package (Revelle, 2020).

We also practice using the Spearman Brown prophecy formula, estimating standardized coefficient alpha, and briefly practice estimating the reliability of difference scores. These are more fully explained in Bandalos (2018).

9.1 Coefficient alpha

The equation that is often presented for calculating coefficient alpha is \[ \alpha = \text{estimated }\rho_{cc'} = \frac{k}{k-1}\left(1 - \frac{\sum\sigma_{i}^2}{\sigma_{c}^2}\right)\text{,} \] where \(\rho_{cc'}\) is the reliability coefficient, \(k\) is the number of items, \(i\) is the index for each item, and \(c\) represents the composite score.64 This calculation is convenient because it is easy to perform: All we need is the number of items, the variance of each item, and the variance of the composite score.

It is not very intuitive, however. A more intuitive formula is

\[ \alpha = \frac{k}{k-1}\left(\frac{2\sum_{i\neq{j}}\sigma_{ij}}{\sigma_{c}^2}\right)\text{,} \] where \(j\) is another item index, used to distinguish it from any other item in the covariance, \(\sigma_{ij}\). Inside the parentheses, we have the proportion of the sum of the item covariances to the composite variance. This proportion, after it is corrected with \(\frac{k}{k-1}\), is an estimate of the proportion of the composite-score variance that is explained by true-score variance, \(\frac{\sigma_{T}^2}{\sigma_{C}^2}\).

Computationally, however, it is more work to calculate the covariances between each item pair, \(\sigma_{ij}\), than it is to calculate each item’s variance, \(\sigma_{i}^2\), simply because as we have more items, we have progressively fewer item variances (\(k\) to be exact) than we do covariances (\(k^2 - k\), or \(k(k-1)\), to be exact). With this, a formula for coefficient alpha that is both computationally easy and intuitive is \[ \alpha = \frac{k}{k-1}\left(\frac{\sigma_{c}^2-\sum\sigma_{i}^2}{\sigma_{c}^2}\right)\text{.}\]

The numerator is still the sum of the covariances, \(2\sum\sigma_{ij}\), because the covariances are what are left over in the covariance matrix after we remove the diagonal, which are the item variances,\(\sum\sigma_{i}^2\). For many of us, this is the easiest equation for coefficient alpha to commit to memory.

9.1.1 How coefficient alpha works

Let’s look into the rationale for coefficient alpha and why we’re thinking about this proportion of summed covariances to total (composite) variance. Recall from an earlier chapter that the variance of a composite is the sum of the individual variances and covariances, \(\sigma^2_C = \sum^{k}_{i=1}\sigma^2_{X_i} + \sum^{j=k}_{i<j}2\sigma_{(X_i,X_j)}\). We can see this in a variance-covariance matrix. Let’s use one with three items:

\[\begin{bmatrix} \sigma^2_{X_1} & \sigma_{X_1X_2} & \sigma_{X_1X_3} \\ \sigma_{X_2X_1} & \sigma^2_{X_2} & \sigma_{X_2X_3} \\ \sigma_{X_3X_1} & \sigma_{X_3X_2} & \sigma^2_{X_3} \end{bmatrix}\]

We can also think of each item as a composite score, consisting of \(T\) and \(E\). With this, we can expand the matrix for one item as this:

\[\sigma^2_{X_1} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma^2_{T_1} & \sigma_{T_1E_1} \\ \sigma_{E_1T_1} & \sigma^2_{E_1} \end{bmatrix}\] Because of the CTT assumption that \(\rho_{ET} = 0\),

\[\sigma^2_{X_1} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma^2_{T_1} & \sigma_{T_1E_1} \\ \sigma_{E_1T_1} & \sigma^2_{E_1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma^2_{T_1} & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma^2_{E_1} \end{bmatrix} \] We can replace \(\sigma^2_{X_1}\) in the matrix, expanding it to include these four terms (in boldface below). We can also do this for each item’s variance term and then put them in the matrix as well. \[ \begin{bmatrix} \pmb{\sigma^2_{X_1}} & \sigma_{X_1X_2} & \sigma_{X_1X_3} \\ \sigma_{X_2X_1} & \sigma^2_{X_2} & \sigma_{X_2X_3} \\ \sigma_{X_3X_1} & \sigma_{X_3X_2} & \sigma^2_{X_3} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \pmb{\sigma^2_{T_1}} & \pmb{0} & ? & ? & ? & ? \\ \pmb{0} & \pmb{\sigma^2_{E_1}} & ? & ? & ? & ? \\ ? & ? & \sigma^2_{T_2} & 0 & ? & ? \\ ? & ? & 0 & \sigma_{E_2}^2 & ? & ? \\ ? & ? & ? & ? & \sigma_{T_3}^2 & 0 \\ ? & ? & ? & ? & 0 & \sigma_{E_3}^2 \end{bmatrix}\] We still have a lot of covariances to deal with, as we can see by all the question marks in the matrix. With our CTT assumptions, though, our job is not so hard. First, let’s recall that the covariance of a composite score is the sum of the covariances of all possible pairs, \(\sigma_{(X + Y, Z + W)} = \sigma_{X,Z} + \sigma_{X, W} + \sigma_{Y,Z} + \sigma_{Y,W}\). With this, when we have two composite scores made up of true scores and error, we have \[\sigma_{(T_1 + E_1, T_2 + E_2)} = \sigma_{T_1,T_2} + \sigma_{T_1, E_2} + \sigma_{E_1,T_2} + \sigma_{E_1,E_2} \text{.}\] Because of the assumptions of classical test theory, the errors do not correlate with the true scores or with another composite’s error, \(\rho_{E_{i}E_{j}} = 0\) and \(\rho_{E_{i}T_{j}} = 0\), so we can simplify this to \[\sigma_{_{(T_1 + E_1, T_2 + E_2)}} = \sigma_{_{T_1,T_2}} + 0 + 0 + 0 \text{.}\] With Items 1 and 2, we can replace these corresponding question marks on both sides of the diagonal in the matrix:

\[\sigma^2_{C} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma^2_{T_1} & 0 & \pmb{\sigma_{T_1T_2}} & \pmb{0} & ? & ? \\ 0 & \sigma^2_{E_1} & \pmb{0} & \pmb{0} & ? & ? \\ \pmb{\sigma_{T_2T_1}} & \pmb{0} & \sigma^2_{T_2} & 0 & ? & ? \\ \pmb{0} & \pmb{0} & 0 & \sigma_{E_2}^2 & ? & ? \\ ? & ? & ? & ? & \sigma_{T_3}^2 & 0 \\ ? & ? & ? & ? & 0 & \sigma_{E_3}^2 \end{bmatrix}\]

We can do this same thing with all other covariances. Again, anything that covaries with error will be a \(0\). What are left are the covariances of true scores, \(\sigma_{T_iT_j}\).

\[\sigma^2_{C} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma^2_{T_1} & 0 & \sigma_{T_1T_2} & 0 & \sigma_{T_1T_3} & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma^2_{E_1} & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ \sigma_{T_2T_1} & 0 & \sigma^2_{T_2} & 0 & \sigma^2_{T} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \sigma_{E_2}^2 & 0 & 0 \\ \sigma_{T_3T_1} & 0 & \sigma_{T_3T_2} & 0 & \sigma^2_{T_3} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \sigma_{E_3}^2 \end{bmatrix}\]

Furthermore, if we have at least an essentially tau-equivalent model, the variances of the true scores are all assumed to be equal, \(\sigma_{T_j}^2=\sigma_{T_i}^2\). Even better, because the relationship between \(T_i\) and \(T_j\) is perfectly linear, with \(b = 1\) in \(T_j = a + bT_i\), we can simplify the \(\sigma_{T_iT_j}\) terms to be \(\sigma_{T_i}^2\). This is because the correlation between any two true scores in an essentially tau-equivalent model will be \(1\), which along with \(\sigma_{T_j}^2=\sigma_{T_i}^2\) means that the covariance of any pair will be the same as the variance of any true score, \(\sigma_{T_iT_j}=\sigma_{T_i^2}\).65 Therefore, with a model that is at least essentially tau-equivalent, we can show that the variance of a composite score created from three items is composed of true-score variance and error-variance in the following way:

\[\sigma^2_{C} = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma^2_{\pmb{T_i}} & 0 & \sigma^2_{\pmb{T_i}} & 0 & \sigma^2_{\pmb{T_i}} & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma^2_{E_1} & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ \sigma^2_{\pmb{T_i}} & 0 & \sigma^2_{\pmb{T_i}} & 0 & \sigma^2_{\pmb{T_i}} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \sigma_{E_2}^2 & 0 & 0 \\ \sigma^2_{\pmb{T_i}}& 0 &\sigma^2_{\pmb{T_i}} & 0 & \sigma^2_{\pmb{T_i}} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \sigma_{E_3}^2 \end{bmatrix}\] If our task is to separate true-score variance from error-variance, we can look to the off-diagonal of our matrix. In other words, we can use the true-score components of the item covariances. All that is left in the covariances is true-score variance. This is only true if the assumptions of the true-score model hold and if we have at least an essentially tau equivalent model. If an essentially tau equivalent model cannot be assumed, these covariances will be lower than their counterpart true-score variances on the diagonal.

Although we can calculate the covariances of all the items, we can also get away with calculating the total variance of the composite and subtracting the sum of our item variances. This will give us the covariances: \(\sigma_{c}^2-\sum\sigma_{i}^2\), which is the numerator in main part of coefficient alpha, \(\frac{\sigma_{c}^2-\sum\sigma_{i}^2}{\sigma_{c}^2}\).

So, what we have is a proportion of the total variance that consists of the true-score variance—well, almost anyway. We still have to account for those three true-score variances on the diagonal, right, because we just subtracted them along with their errors with the \(-\sum\sigma_{i}^2\) term. How many true-score variance terms are there in total? Looking at our matrix we just created, we see we have \(9\) \(\sigma^2_{T_i}\) terms. In other words, we have \(k^2\) variances. Right now, our numerator only includes \(6\), or \(k^2-k\) or \(k(k-1)\), of the \(9\) true-score variance terms. To go from \(k(k-1) = 3(3-1) = 6\) back to \(k^2 = 3^2= 9\), we need an adjuster. This is the purpose of our first term in coefficient alpha: \(\frac{k}{k-1}*k(k-1) = k^2\). So, with the equation for coefficient alpha, we recover our \(9\) item-level true-score variance estimates. Under the assumption that the items making up the composite test score fit an essentially tau equivalent model,

\[\begin{eqnarray*} \alpha &=& \frac{k}{k-1}\left(\frac{\sigma_{c}^2-\sum\sigma_{i}^2}{\sigma_{c}^2}\right) \\[5pt] &=& \frac{3}{3-1}\left(\frac{\sum\sigma_{T_i}^2 \text{ on the off-diagonal}}{\sigma_{c}^2}\right) = 1.5\left(\frac{6\sigma_{T_i}^2}{\sigma_{c}^2}\right) = \frac{9\sigma_{T_i}^2}{\sigma_{c}^2}\\[5pt] &=& \frac{\sigma_{T}^2}{\sigma_{c}^2} \text{ .} \end{eqnarray*}\]

It is a safe bet that our researcher-developed tests do not have essentially tau-equivalent models. With this, we cannot assume that \(\alpha = \rho_{cc'}\). If \(b \neq 1\) in the linear relationship between the item true scores, \(T_j = a + bT_i\), the covariances will be lower than the variances. Because of this, we generally accept that coefficient alpha is a lower-bound estimate of the true reliability coefficient, \(\alpha \leq \rho_{cc'}\).66

9.2 Using R to estimate internal-consistency reliability coefficient

Coefficient alpha provides one estimate of internal-consistency reliability.67 First, let’s use R with some simulated hypothetical data—the data from our previous chapter on classical test theory—to compare our calculation of coefficient alpha with our omniscient calculation of reliability. After that, we’ll use two packages to do the work for us with another data set that we used in previous chapter.

9.2.1 A demonstration of coefficient alpha using hypothetical data

Let’s use our hypothetical data from the previous chapter. These include five tests (or sub-tests, or items). We used these to demonstrate the four possible models.

  1. Tests 1 and 2 fit a parallel model. Their true scores had the same means and variances, and their error variances (and therefore observed variances) were the same.
  2. Tests 1 and 3 fit a tau-equivalent model. Their true scores had the same means and variances.
  3. Tests 1 and 4 fit an essentially tau-equivalent model. Their true scores had different means but were the same in their variances.
  4. Tests 1 and 5 fit a congeneric model. Their true scores differed in means and variances.

I’ll wager (because I simulated these data) that we can examine Tests 1 through 4 as sub-tests that together meet the requirements of an essentially tau-equivalent model. Let’s read our data in and then calculate the total observed scores (the composite), the true scores, and the error scores. These are hypothetical—we’d never be able to know a person’s true score or error score. But, we can compare the theoretical ratio, \(\frac{\sigma_{T}^2}{\sigma_{C}^2}\), with the results of the formula for coefficient alpha to see how close we can get.

Take a look at the data:

Then, let’s use each object to apply the sum() function across items to calculate each person’s composite score for each. nums <- (1:4) # Add + 1 to include the 5th one, which is congeneric.

CTT_data$totC <- apply(CTT_data[ ,obs_inames], 1, sum)
CTT_data$totE <- apply(CTT_data[ ,err_inames], 1, sum)
CTT_data$totT <- apply(CTT_data[ ,tru_inames], 1, sum)
# Let's just make sure they got attached to our data frame and that they look correct:

The totC variable is the composite score based on the first four sub-tests. Let’s take a look at \(\frac{\sigma_{T}^2}{\sigma_{C}^2}\):

var(CTT_data$totT) / var(CTT_data$totC)
## [1] 0.8567942

With our omniscient vantage, we know we have a true reliability of \(\rho_{cc'} =\) 0.86. Let’s see if coefficient alpha yields a similar result. If the sub-tests fit at least an essentially-tau-equivalent model, the value should be the same. We’re using the length(obs_inames) to count how many items. We know it’s \(4\), but just in case.

k <- length(obs_inames) 
var_items <- apply(CTT_data[ ,obs_inames], 2, var)
var_c <- var(CTT_data$totC)
round(var_items,2)
##    X1    X2    X3    X4 
## 13.00 13.01 15.01 19.00

Let’s plug these into our equation for coefficient alpha:68

my_alpha <- k/(k-1)*(var_c - sum(var_items))/var_c
my_alpha
## [1] 0.8571967

The result, \(\alpha =\) 0.86, is very similar to our previous estimate, \(\rho_{cc'} =\) 0.86. This is because our first four items fit at least an essentially-tau-equivalent model.

9.2.1.1 With data that fit only a congeneric model

Let’s try this whole procedure again, but with Items 2 through 5. We know from our use of these data in the previous chapter that Test 5 (or “Item 5”) fits at best a congeneric model with at least one of the other tests (Test 1, and likely the others). In the linear relationship between the item true scores, \(T_j = a + bT_5\), \(b \neq 1\). The variance of the true scores on Item 5 are also different from the variances of the other items.69

nums <- (2:5) # NOW, we're looking at Items 2 through 5.
obs_inames <- c(paste0("X",nums))
tru_inames <- c(paste0("T",nums))
err_inames <- c(paste0("E",nums))

CTT_data$totC <- apply(CTT_data[ ,obs_inames], 1, sum)
CTT_data$totE <- apply(CTT_data[ ,err_inames], 1, sum)
CTT_data$totT <- apply(CTT_data[ ,tru_inames], 1, sum)
var(CTT_data$totT) / var(CTT_data$totC)
## [1] 0.8164755

The real reliability, \(\rho_{cc'} =\) 0.82, is a little lower than the earlier set of four items. Let’s calculate alpha:

k <- length(obs_inames) 
var_items <- apply(CTT_data[ ,obs_inames], 2, var)
var_c <- var(CTT_data$totC)
my_alpha <- k/(k-1)*(var_c - sum(var_items))/var_c
my_alpha
## [1] 0.7843741

We can see that \(\alpha =\) 0.78 is lower than our true reliability, \(\rho_{cc'} =\) 0.82,. This is because our data do not fit at least an essentially-tau-equivalent model.

9.2.2 Using R packages to calculate alpha and more

Let’s take a look at the data we saw in the chapter on item analysis. Notice that we have dichotomously-scored item level data and that we already have a total score. While we’re here, let’s create an object that contains only our item data. This will make it easier to perform functions on.

dat <- read.csv("Week06_Scored_reponses.csv")

9.3 Spearman-Brown prophecy formula

The Spearman-Brown prophecy formula is great for seeing how many more (or fewer) items we will need to achieve a desired reliability.70

9.3.1 Calculation

To estimate the new reliability, given a factor of new items,\(k\),71 and the original test’s reliability, \(\rho_{_{CC'old}}\): \[ \rho_{_{CC'new}} = \frac{k\rho_{_{CC'old}}}{1 + (k - 1)\rho_{_{CC'old}} } \] For instance, if we wanted to estimate the new reliability if we doubled the test’s length and its original reliability were \(.60\),

\[\begin{eqnarray*} \rho_{_{CC'new}} &=& \frac{k\rho_{_{CC'old}}}{1 + (k - 1)\rho_{_{CC'old}} }\\[5pt] \rho_{_{CC'new}} &=& \frac{2(.60)}{1 + .60} \\[5pt] \rho_{_{CC'new}} &=& .75 \end{eqnarray*} \]

This estimate is based on the assumption that the new items fit a parallel model with the existing items.

We can also rearrange the terms in the equation to find out the factor by which we would need to multiply our number of items to achieve a desired reliability.

\[\begin{eqnarray*} k &=& \frac{\rho_{_{CC'new}}(1 - \rho_{_{CC'old}})}{\rho_{_{CC'old}}(1 - \rho_{_{CC'new}}) }\\[5pt] k &=& \frac{.75(1 - .60)}{.60(1 - .75) }\\[5pt] k &=& \frac{0.30}{0.15} = 2.00 \end{eqnarray*} \] We’d need twice as many items than our original test. For instance if we had 10 items, we’d need 10 more parallel-model items.

9.3.2 Using the CTT package

The CTT package has a function called spearman.brown() to estimate, of all things, the results from using the Spearman-Brown prophecy formula. We set input = to ask what our new reliability would be if we lengthened or shortened our test. If we doubled our test, we’d achieve this estimate if the new items were parallel to those we already have:

library(CTT)
relachieved_if_doubled <- spearman.brown(0.60, input = 2, n.or.r = "n")
relachieved_if_doubled
## $r.new
## [1] 0.75

So, with double the number of items, we’d have a reliability of 0.75.

The other use is to identify how many more items, of a parallel model, we need to obtain a desired reliability. For this, we need to specify what our original reliability estimate is, such as \(0.60\), and the desired reliability, such as \(0.80\):72

library(CTT)
factorNeeded <- spearman.brown(0.60, input = .80, n.or.r = "r")
factorNeeded
## $n.new
## [1] 2.666667

Apparently, we need to increase our test length by a factor of 2.67. If our original test had 10 items, we need a total of 27 items to achieve a reliability of .80. We’d better get to work!

9.4 Standardized coefficient alpha

You may have noticed in the output from the psych package’s alpha() function that there are two reports of coefficient alpha. The one we are typically interested in is labeled as “raw_alpha”, as this is the estimate from the equations we’ve looked at so far. Adjacent to that, there is “std.alpha”. The standardized alpha is valuable when the sub-tests used to calculate the composite scores have very different variances.

For instance, if a researcher were to create a composite language-arts score, we might see three sub-tests, such as students’

  1. grade point averages in language arts classes,
  2. test scores from their states’ language arts assessments, and
  3. their scores on the language section of the SAT.

The scales of these three item-level variables in their raw form would dramatically differ from each other. The scale of the GPA scores would be from \(0\) to \(4\)73, whereas the scales of the large-scale tests will be much larger, such as from \(200\) to \(800\).74 In these situations, the researcher typically standardizes these scores before summing them into a composite score. This will transform all of the items’ variances to \(1\).

Before standardizing them, the researcher may wish to examine reliability. In that situation, the variances will be very different from each other. In this situation, we use the standardized coefficient alpha.

9.4.1 Calculation of standardized coefficient alpha

There are three steps to calculating standardized alpha:

  1. Calculate the correlations among all pairs of items, \(r_{i,j}\).
  2. Calculate the average of these correlations, \(\bar{r}_{ij}\).
  3. Apply the Spearman-Brown Prophecy Formula on \(\bar{r}_{ij}\), with \(k\) being the number of items.

Here’s an example: Let’s say we already have a covariance matrix of GPA, State-test scores, and SAT scores from a previous researcher’s report.

\[\begin{bmatrix}0.16&12.25&18.37 \\12.25&10000&7916.67 \\18.37&7916.67&10000 \\\end{bmatrix}\]

Let’s do Step 1 based on what we have. We have covariance data, so let’s create this matrix in R using the matrix() function. The matrix() function takes a vector of numbers (here, with a length of 9), separated by commas, and then puts them into a matrix if we specify the number of rows with the nrow = argument (or the ncol = argument). We can also name the columns and rows.75 We can visually line up the columns and rows to help us see the matrix, but we could have just as easily included all of these numbers in a single line. This is one way to enter small data sets into R.

S <- matrix( c( 0.16,    12.25,    18.37,
               12.25,    10000,  7916.67,
               18.37,  7916.67,   10000),
            nrow = 3, byrow = TRUE)
rownames(S) <- colnames(S) <- c("GPA", "State_test", "SAT")
S
##              GPA State_test      SAT
## GPA         0.16      12.25    18.37
## State_test 12.25   10000.00  7916.67
## SAT        18.37    7916.67 10000.00

A standard symbol for covariance matrices is S. For correlation matrices, we use R. From the covariance matrix, we can get a correlation matrix. We can do this by hand, or we can use R’s cov2cor() function:76

R <- cov2cor(S)
R
##                GPA State_test      SAT
## GPA        1.00000   0.306250 0.459250
## State_test 0.30625   1.000000 0.791667
## SAT        0.45925   0.791667 1.000000

Of course, if we had the raw data, we could have simply used the cor() function. Now that we have the correlation matrix, let’s go to Step 2 and get the mean of the correlations. Here’s a quick way: First, we remove the diagonal because these are all \(1\)s. We can do this with the diag() function and assign these all NAs.

diag(R) <- NA
R
##                GPA State_test      SAT
## GPA             NA   0.306250 0.459250
## State_test 0.30625         NA 0.791667
## SAT        0.45925   0.791667       NA

Now, we perform the mean function on what’s left in of the matrix:

diag(R) <- NA
r_bar <- mean(R, na.rm = TRUE)
r_bar
## [1] 0.5190557

The mean of all the correlations is \(\bar{r}_{ij}=\) 0.52. For Step 3, we’ll use the Spearman Brown prophecy formula using \(\bar{r}_{ij}\) in place of \(\rho_{_{CC'old}}\). This makes sense because the variances are all 1 in the correlation matrix R so the covariances that we use in coefficient alpha are the item correlations. Here, \(k\) is the number of items because we can think of \(\bar{r}_{ij}\) as our best guess about the reliability of one of the sub-tests.

\[\begin{eqnarray*} \text{standardized }\alpha &=& \frac{k\bar{r}_{ij}}{1 + (k - 1)\bar{r}_{ij} }\\[5pt] \end{eqnarray*} \] Let’s do that:

k <- 3 
alpha_std <- (k*r_bar)/(1+(k-1)*r_bar)
round( alpha_std, 3)
## [1] 0.764

We can, of course, also use the psych package’s alpha() function and retrieve the same result. By the way, that function also accepts covariance matrices. Try using it on S.

9.5 Reliability of difference scores

Sometimes, researchers wish to report the reliability of difference scores. Although there are better ways to do this than with CTT, here is how we’d do it. Let’s say that we already know the reliability of the two tests, one a pre-, the other a post-intervention test and we have only the total scores. We can estimate the variance of the two tests, their correlation, and plug them into the formula.

\[\begin{eqnarray*} \rho_{_{DD'}} &=& \frac{\rho_{XX'}\sigma_{X}^2 + \rho_{YY'}\sigma_{Y}^2 - 2\rho_{XY}\sigma_{X}\sigma_{Y}} {\sigma_{X}^2 + \sigma_{Y}^2 - 2\rho_{XY}\sigma_{X}\sigma_{Y} }\\[5pt] \end{eqnarray*} \] Let’s do this in R, with the Week06_DiffScoresEg.csv data and with the reliability of X and Y already known:

difdat<- read.csv("Week06_DiffScoresEg.csv")
relX <- .953
relY <- .926
varX <- var(difdat$X)
varY <- var(difdat$Y)
sX   <- sqrt(varX)
sY    <- sqrt(varY)
covXY <- cov(difdat$X,difdat$Y)
corXY <- cor(difdat$X,difdat$Y)

numer <- relX*varX + relY*varY - 2*corXY*sX*sY
denom <- varX + varY - 2*corXY*sX*sY

relDif <- numer/denom
relDif
## [1] 0.456461

That’s unsatisfyingly low. This is because we had to subtract the covariances.

We could also create a function to do this. We can then reuse this with our data we just looked at. Also, we can use the data in Bandalos’s discussion of this topic in Table 8.3 of her text.

rel_differ <- function(rel_x, rel_y, var_x, var_y, cor_xy){
  sX     <-   sqrt(var_x)
  sY     <-   sqrt(var_y)
  numer  <- rel_x*var_x + rel_y*var_y - 2*cor_xy*sX*sY
  denom  <- var_x + var_y - 2*cor_xy*sX*sY
  relDif <- numer/denom
  round(relDif, 3)
}

rel_differ(.953, .926, var(difdat$X), var(difdat$Y), cor(difdat$X, difdat$Y) )
## [1] 0.456

Here are the calculations in Bandalos, Table 8.3:

rel_differ(.80, .90, 3, 5, .20)
## [1] 0.829
rel_differ(.80, .90, 3, 5, .40)
## [1] 0.776
rel_differ(.80, .90, 3, 5, .60)
## [1] 0.672

The reliability of difference scores will always be lower than the reliability of the individual tests. Researchers can avoid having to calculate this estimate by using different types of analyses that do not require the calculation of difference scores. One option is to use one of the variables, such as the pre-intervention scores, as a covariate in their analyses. In this way, we partial out the variance due to the first measurement. Another approach is to estimate the difference effect by treating the two scores as multivariate data. It turns out that with two scores, we will have a difference-in-differences approach.77
