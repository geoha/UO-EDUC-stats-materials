Chapter 11 Generalizability Theory

In this chapter, we expand our understanding of reliability to include multiple factors, or facets as they are called in the generalizability-theory framework (Brennan, 2001; Shavelson & Webb, 1991). Up to now, we have only addressed persons’ scores across items or across raters. In the last chapter, the raters provided a single rating for each person. However, in many situations, raters provide a rating for several tasks. In such a design, we have two facets, one for raters and one for tasks, such as items or test-rubric categories. Regular old classical test theory does not account for this complexity. Generalizability theory (G theory) does.

When we use a G-theory approach, we conduct two steps: a generalizability-study (G-study), and a decision study (D-study). A G-study is for examining the partitioning of variance. A D-study provides information for making decisions (or perhaps confirming decisions) in the operational study—that is, when we use the instrument in practice.

The results of a G-study reveal how much variance in the scores is estimated to be explained by

  • the object of measurement (usually persons),
  • each of the facets,
    • including the interactions among facets, and
    • all interactions with the object of measurement except the highest-order interaction, and
  • error (or residuals), which is confounded with the highest order interaction in the study.

For a one-facet study, say with raters who score persons, this would be the variance components of

  • persons (\(\sigma_{p}^2\)),
  • raters (\(\sigma_{r}^2\)), and
  • the person-by-rater interaction confounded with error (\(\sigma_{pr,e}^2\)).

For a two-facet study, say with raters who score persons on several items (or several categories on a rubric), the sources of variation would be

  • persons (\(\sigma_{p}^2\)),
  • raters (\(\sigma_{r}^2\)),
  • items (\(\sigma_{i}^2\)),
  • persons-by-raters interaction (\(\sigma_{pr}^2\)),
  • persons-by-items interaction (\(\sigma_{pi}^2\)),
  • raters-by-items interaction (\(\sigma_{ri}^2\)), and
  • the person-by-rater-by-item interaction confounded with error (\(\sigma_{pri,e}^2\)).

This approach to examining the source of variation in scores is one step beyond how we think about variance in CTT, in which only the true-score variance (i.e., that of the object of measurement) and error are of concern. In a G-study, if we have a test with items that vary in difficulty, say if we have some very easy and very difficult items, we can estimate this variance. In CTT, the parallel and tau equivalent models assume that all of the items are of equal difficulty. Realistically, we expect some variation in item difficulty, so we are not usually overly concerned with this facet, though we still seek to have a large proportion of the variance be attributed to differences among the persons.88

However, if instead of items we have a rater facet and if we intend to use the scores from these ratings to make criterion-referenced decisions, such as for diagnosis to determine the need for treatment or for judgments about achievement of the level of material learned in a curriculum, we should pay close attention to the proportion of variance that is explained by raters. Some raters may differ in how lenient or stringent they are and if this variation is large, the validity of our interpretations of the scores will be questioned.

In contrast, if all we are interested in when we interpret the scores is how the persons compare to each other (such as in a performance competition), this variation among raters is of little concern. What will be a concern with both items and raters will be the idiosyncratic person-by-rater or person-by-item variation. If, for example, a particular rater is harsh with a particular person but lenient with other persons, the legitimacy of the scores is at stake. This type of interaction in a one facet design will be confounded with error. In a two-facet design, such as when raters rate persons on several items (or tasks or occasions), we can detect this type of bias in a G-study.

In a D-study, we are either interested in a generalizability coefficient (\(G\)) or a phi-coefficient (\(\phi\)).89 A G-coefficient is analogous to reliability in classical test theory and is appropriate when the interpretations of the test scores will concern how the persons rank against each other. In other words, just as is the case with coefficient alpha and the ICC coefficients for consistency (\(\text{ICC}_{Ck}\)), the G-coefficient is for norm-referenced interpretations.

A phi-coefficient is not, strictly speaking, reliability as it concerns agreement in addition to consistency, just as the ICCs for agreement (\(\text{ICC}_{A1}\)) do. Because it is not really reliability, the phi-coefficient is sometimes called the dependability index.

In a D-study, we can also estimate the G- or phi-coefficient if we used a different number of facets. This is analogous to the Spearman Brown prophecy formula in CTT. This is valuable for our decisions in the operational study. For instance, in our pilot-test study with a set of raters, we might discover that our raters vary excessively in their stringency (which would be important for criterion-referenced score interpretations). If we have a set threshold for what an acceptable phi-coefficient should be, and we are below that threshold with our current number of raters, we can estimate how many more raters we need to hire.

Similar to the ICC with inter-rater reliability and agreement, G-theory uses an ANOVA framework for estimating the variance components. There are two methods for estimating variance components. The first uses the traditional ordinary-least-squares estimation that we employ in a linear model, such as with the lm() function in R. The second uses maximum-likelihood estimation methods to estimate the variance components. We will begin with a single-facet design example, using the typical ANOVA approach. After this, we will practice with multiple-facet designs using the VCA package (Schuetzenmeister & Dufey, 2020), which uses maximum-likelihood estimation methods.

Before we try out G theory with two or more facets, let’s see how it operates with a single facet, raters. Let’s use the data from Table 10.2 in Bandalos’ chapter:

We now have an object, called AnTab that is our ANOVA table. We can use indexing to extract the values from that table and assign them to objects. For example, we’re assigning the value 4.816 to the MSp object, which is the mean-squares of the persons.

MSp    <- (AnTab["person",    "Mean Sq"])
MSr    <- (AnTab["rater",     "Mean Sq"])
MSpr_e <- (AnTab["Residuals", "Mean Sq"])

To calculate the variance components, we also need the number of persons and the number of raters. We use the length() function to count the number of levels in each of the two factors and assign them to objects.91 In G theory, a level is called a condition.

n_p <- length(levels(dat$person))
n_r <- length(levels(dat$rater))

We can now use the mean-squares (i.e., the variances) and the number of persons and number of raters to estimate the variance components:

VCp <- (MSp - MSpr_e)/n_r
VCr <- (MSr - MSpr_e)/n_p
VCpr_e <- MSpr_e

The variance components of persons, \(\hat{\sigma}_{p}^2\) (which in our R code is labeled as VCp) is the expected variance of the persons across a single condition of the other facet (raters). Because there are several raters, the \(MSp\) right now includes the variance across the collections of these raters. Dividing by the number of raters, n_r, estimates this as the average variance component per rater, which is an estimate of the variance component in the population of raters (or universe of raters).

We can assign all of these variance components,\(\hat{\sigma}_{p}^2\), \(\hat{\sigma}_{r}^2\), and \(\hat{\sigma}_{e}^2\) to an object, VarComps. This is now a vector of values, so we can sum those values to get the total variance TotVar and divide the elements of the VarCompos vector to get each variance component’s proportion of the total variance.

VarComps <- c(VCp, VCr, VCpr_e)
TotVar   <- sum(VarComps)
PropVar  <- VarComps/TotVar

We can now create a generalizability study (G-study) report. First we create an object for the source of variance, which we’ll call Source, with p, r, pr,e, and Total being the source of variance in the report. Source <- c("p", "r", "pr,e", "Total")

11.1.2 D-study

A decision study, or D-study, includes the calculations that we use to interpret the reliability or dependability of our scores, which in turn will aid us in making judgments or decisions about our instrument and scoring procedures. The first step involves estimating the G- and phi-coefficients. The G-coefficient is for relative decisions and is identical to coefficient alpha or \(\text{ICC}_{Ck}\) if we have a fully crossed one-facet design (if we have data about every condition of the facet—such as every rater—for every person). The phi-coefficient is for absolute decisions and is identical to \(\text{ICC}_{Ak}\) if we only have one facet and the data are fully crossed.

11.1.2.1 G-coefficient

Now that we have the expected variance components, we can estimate the reliability (G-coefficient). The formula is \[\begin{eqnarray*} G = \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{p}^2 }{\hat{\sigma}_{p}^2 + \hat{\sigma}_{REL}^2} \text{ .} \end{eqnarray*}\]

We see that the G-coefficient is estimated as a ratio of variance. The numerator is the estimated variance component of the object of measurement (persons). The denominator is that variance component plus the estimated error variance, as it is defined for relative decisions. With this particular example, this denominator is equivalent to the composite-score variance in CTT.

With this, the first thing we need to do is calculate the error. In a G-study with relative decisions, the error term, \(\hat{\sigma}_{REL}^2\), is all the sources of variation that have an effect on persons’ relative standing in the scores.92 Only the variance components that include interactions with the object of measurement—persons in this case—are included in this. In our one-facet design, the variance component of the raters is not included as error but the person-rater interaction, \(\hat{\sigma}_{pr, e}^2\), is. An easy way to determine whether to include the variance component in the relative error term is to see if the name of the variance component includes our symbol for the object of measurement, which is \(_p\) in our case. If the variance component does not include an interaction with persons, the component is not part of the relative error term. Here is the equation with our single-facet example: \[\hat{\sigma}_{REL}^2 = \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{pr,e}^2}{n_r}\] We can calculate this in R:

Err_Rel <- VCpr_e/n_r
Err_Rel
## [1] 0.3223906

One thing to note is that if we take the square root of this error variance, we have the estimated standard error of measurement, \(\hat{\sigma}_{REL}\).

Now, we can perform the calculation:

\[\begin{eqnarray*} \text{G} &=& \frac{ {\\1.283} }{ {\\1.283 + 0.322} }\\[5pt] &=& {\\0.799} \end{eqnarray*}\] Doing this in R, we can use this code:

G     <- VCp/(VCp + Err_Rel)
round(G,3)
## [1] 0.799

The G-coefficient reliability of the scores is .799. If we used an ICC approach, with the irr package, we will observe the same results, though we would have to reshape the data back to wide format. Let’s do that using the pivot_wider() function from the tidyr package (Wickham, 2020c).93

11.1.2.2 Phi-coefficient

We can calculate the phi-coefficient in a similar manner. \[\begin{eqnarray*} \phi = \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{p}^2 }{\hat{\sigma}_{p}^2 + \hat{\sigma}_{ABS}^2} \end{eqnarray*}\]

The denominator includes an error variance for absolute decisions. \(\hat{\sigma}_{ABS}^2\) is all the sources of variation besides that of our object of measurement.94 So, the error in our phi-coefficient includes the variance component of the raters. Here is the formula for the error variance with our one-facet design: \[\hat{\sigma}_{ABS}^2 = \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{pr,e}^2}{n_r} + \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{r}^2}{n_r}\] And here is our calculation in R:

Err_Abs <- VCpr_e/n_r + VCr/n_r
Err_Abs
## [1] 0.3518519

If the raters differ dramatically in their degree of stringency or leniency, this variability will be high and the amount of absolute error variance component will be high.

\[\begin{eqnarray*} \phi &=& \frac{ {\\1.283} }{ {\\1.283 + 0.352} }\\[5pt] &=& {\\0.785} \end{eqnarray*}\] Doing this in R, we can use this code:

Phi     <- VCp/(VCp + Err_Abs)
round(Phi,3)
## [1] 0.785

The dependability index is \(\phi = {.785}\) . Let’s see if we get the same result from our \(\text{ICC}_{Ak}\) estimate:

library(irr)

11.1.2.3 Projecting an optimal number of conditions in a facet

In the earlier discussions of reliability, we used the Spearman Brown prophecy formula to project how many more items (or a multiplicative factor of how many more items) we would need to achieve a desired level of reliability. We can do the same thing in generalizability theory but we have more than just items. We can have multiple facets. For now, let’s use a single facet, raters, and examine how the G-coefficient will change if we add or remove raters:

Let’s create a vector of the number of raters, say if we had 1 rater, 2 raters, 3 (which we do now), 4, and so forth. We can call this Dn_r to remind us that this is the number of raters in a decision study.


 The number of raters does not have to range from 1 to 7. That number depends on how many is meaningful in our study. In fact, we may wish to not report a D-study with projected different number of raters and simply report the G- or phi-coefficient we obtained with however many raters we have already committed to. This would be the natural thing to do if we do not plan to implement any more changes in our design and simply need to report the reliability or dependability. 
Dn_r <- (1:7)
Dn_r
## [1] 1 2 3 4 5 6 7

Now, let’s use Dn_r in our estimations of the error:

Err_Rel <- VCpr_e/Dn_r
Err_Rel
## [1] 0.9671717 0.4835859 0.3223906 0.2417929 0.1934343 0.1611953 0.1381674

Notice that now we have a vector of errors with 7 elements. These correspond with the 7 different rater n-sizes we assigned to the Dn_r object. R knows that if we have a vector, such as Dn_r, and we place it in an equation, such as in VCpr_e/Dn_r, we expect to get the same number of results as there are elements in our vector. Because we now have 7 elements in our Err_Rel vector, the following code will give us 7 G-coefficients:

Err_Rel <- VCpr_e/Dn_r
G       <- VCp/(VCp + Err_Rel)
Dstudy  <- data.frame(N_rater = Dn_r, Err_Rel = round(Err_Rel,3), G_coef = round(G,3))

11.2 A two facet design

Designs with two or more facets are valuable for detecting systematic variation between one facet and the object of measurement (as well as among facets). For instance, if we have raters rating persons on several tasks (or items), we can look for evidence of rater-person bias by examining this interaction’s proportion of variance in the G-study. If the proportion of variance is high (based on our reason-based judgments), it suggests that some raters are lenient with some persons but stringent with others. In other words, we have evidence suggesting that some raters might be biased toward or against some of the examinees. With this, we might consider improving our rater-training procedures. This type of systematic variation was undetectable in our one-facet study because this interaction was confounded with error.

We can also examine the item-person interaction to determine if the test tasks are differentially difficult across persons. For instance, if a subset of persons are differentially familiar with some items’ content compared to the other items, they may find the item easier than we would expect given their performance on the other items.

A large item-person variance proportion may be because of the persons’ experience with the content domain being measured. For example, consider a test given to students and some of those students had attended an educational program that heavily emphasized one portion of the content domain (represented on the test by some items) but ignored other portions of the content domain (represented by the other test items). Compared to the rest of the students, our subset of students will probably perform better on those items measuring the emphasized portion of the content. Similarly, we might think that they perform differentially worse on those items that were ignored in their curriculum because the differential opportunity to learn that portion of the content. If this is the case, the items themselves are not at fault but we might be cautious in our claims about what the students’ scores actually mean.

Another source of item-person variance may be because the items are easier for students based on certain traits that are unrelated to their learning of the content. For example, if our test is measuring an academic skill such as algebra word problems but half of the word problems assume the students understand the rules of American football, those students who are unfamiliar with this particular sport will be at a disadvantage. If this trend is present in a single item, this type of differential difficulty is idiosyncratic and is confounded with error. In contrast, if this trend is present across several items, such as with half of the test, the item-person variance will be high and we should consider inspecting and probably revising our items. A well-developed test would include a diverse set of word-problem scenarios in order to lower this type of item-person variance.

As should be obvious by now, a G-study can provide us with information that can help us investigate how our instrument is functioning. Let’s look at an example, using data reported in Furr’s (2018) discussion of a two-facet design. This example includes a rater facet, an item facet, and persons as the object of measurement. A shortcut in R is to use an asterisk instead of a colon between variables. This will include that interaction and all lower-order interactions and main effects. For instance, person*rater [or (person)*(rater) with this particular package] includes the two main effects, person and rater, and the person-by-rater interaction, person:rater. If we used person*rater*item, all three main effects, all three second order interactions, and the three-way interaction would be included in the model—note that we do not want that in our model because the three-way interaction is the highest order interaction and therefore confounded with error.

The main effect of items explains some (4.86%) of the variance. This is not a big deal if we are only concerned with relative decisions. Some variation is to be expected because the tasks in items likely vary in their difficulty, just as we would expect in an essentially tau-equivalent CTT model in which the item p-values vary. In generalizability theory, the conditions of a random facet, such as the three items here, are assumed to be exchangeable with other possible items that might have been included in the test. Each item is assumed to be a randomly selected task from a population of possible items, or in G-theory terms, the items are exchangeable with other items in a universe of admissible operations. Obviously, not all tasks that are potentially used to elicit some kind of latent trait are going to be of equal difficulty. Still, if we are using our scores to make absolute decisions, and using the phi-coefficient, we prefer a low item variance.

The raters also seem to vary in their levels of stringency or leniency, with \(\hat{\sigma}_{r}^2\) explaining 7.38% of the variance. If we’re only interested in relative decisions, this is usually not a big concern if we consider our raters as representing the population of possible raters, where some are simply more lenient or stringent than others. In G-theory terminology, these raters are exchangeable with other possible raters in the universe of admissible operations. Just as is the case with items, this variance component is what is left over after we consider the rater-person interaction. This is the variation among raters’ average ratings across the persons, in the same way that the difficulty indexes of items (like the p values with dichotomous items) is calculated across all the persons. If we are making absolute decisions, this variability will be important. A large proportion of rater variance means that our estimates of the absolute scores of people will have more uncertainty.

In sum, our primary concerns lie in the person-facet interactions. If those variance components are high, the resulting person scores are explained by some systematic differential difficulty. In our example, it appears that the items are not differentially difficult for persons but that the raters might be differentially stringent in their ratings of persons. There might be some kind of rater-person bias going on.

11.3 Nested designs

Our examples so far have included fully-crossed designs. For instance, in our first example with a one-facet design, every rater rated every person person. This was a persons-crossed-with-raters design (\(p \text{ x } r\)). Our two-facet design had persons crossed with raters and items (\(p \text{ x } r \text{ x } i\)). We can also fit models in which one facet is nested within another. These same models apply to partially nested data.

11.3.1 One-facet nested design

With our one-facet design, if our facet (raters) is nested within persons (\(r:p\)), the facet is confounded with the persons. This is analogous to the one-way ICC models in the previous chapter in which the within-person mean square estimate (\(MS_W\)) contained the mean squares of both the raters and error. In this one-facet design, the rater facet is excluded from the ANOVA. We use persons as the predictor, anovaMM(score ~ (person) , dat), to obtain the ANOVA table. If we fit this model using the VCA package we would use this code:

11.3.2 Two-facet nested design

An example of a nested two-facet design is when we administer a test on two occasions and each occasion includes two items. Let’s say Occasion 1 included Items 1 and 2 and Occasion 2 included Items 3 and 4, making items nested within occasions. This might occur if we had a test-retest design with alternative forms of the test.

This two-facet nested design has persons crossed with items nested within occasions, \(p\text{ x }(i:o)\), design.98 In the VCA package (and in many packages), the forward slash indicates nesting, so occasion/item means items are nested within occasions (or if we read it from left to right, we might say occasions within which are nested items).

11.4 Fixed facets

With models that have more than one facet, we can include a fixed facet. For instance, if we are conducting a study with raters rating persons on several tasks (which we’ll call rubric items) and we are not seeking to claim that these items represent some kind of broader content domain, we can treat the items as fixed. We might do this if we are rating people on their performance on a finite set of tasks for a particular job and we do not seek to draw inferences about how the examinees will do in other jobs that have similar tasks.101

In this type of model, the G- and phi-coefficients will typically be higher than in their random-facets counterpart models because the fixed facet’s variance components are treated differently than they are in the random effects model. They’re no longer considered as error (the exception being their interactions with random facets).

To estimate these, we conduct a G-study as if all the facets were random. Then, for the D-study, we specify the universe-score variance component (analogous to true-score variance in CTT) as including not only the usual person variance (\(\hat{\sigma}_{p}^2\)) but also the person-item interaction variance component (\(\hat{\sigma}_{pi}^2\)). Also, the error terms (\(\hat{\sigma}_{REL}^2\) & \(\hat{\sigma}_{ABS}^2\)) no longer include this variance component (\(\hat{\sigma}_{pi}^2\)). The variance component of the main effect of the fixed facet (\(\hat{\sigma}_{i}^2\)) is also excluded from the absolute error. This makes our numerator in G and phi larger than in the model in which items are treated as random (unless our fixed facet has very little variability to begin with).

Let’s use the example from earlier in the chapter. The G-study will be the same as it was with the random-effects \(p \text{ x } r \text{ x } i\) model, presented in Table 11.6. The D-study will include the following two equations for the error variances.

\[\begin{eqnarray*} \hat{\sigma}_{REL}^2 &=& \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{pr}^2}{n_r} + \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{pri,e}^2}{n_rn_i}\\[20pt] \hat{\sigma}_{ABS}^2 &=& \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{r}^2}{n_r} + \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{ri}^2}{n_rn_i} + \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{pr}^2}{n_r} + \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{pri,e}^2}{n_rn_i} \end{eqnarray*}\] Which, with our variance components yields the following error estimates:

\[\begin{eqnarray*} \hat{\sigma}_{REL}^2 &=& \frac{ {\\0.494} }{ {\\3} } + \frac{ {\\1.417} }{ {\\3*3 }} = {\\0.322}\\[20pt] \hat{\sigma}_{ABS}^2 &=& \frac{ {\\0.439} }{ {\\3} } + \frac{ {\\0.028} }{ {\\3*3 } } + \frac{ {\\0.494} }{ {\\3} } + \frac{ {\\1.417} }{ {\\3*3 }} = {\\0.472} \end{eqnarray*}\] With the variance component of the person-item interaction now being included as a portion of the universe variance, G- and phi-coefficient formulas are

\[\begin{eqnarray*} G &=& \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{p}^2 + \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{pi}^2}{n_i}} {\hat{\sigma}_{p}^2 + \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{pi}^2}{n_i} +\hat{\sigma}_{REL}^2}\\[20pt] \phi &=& \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{p}^2 + \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{pi}^2}{n_i}} {\hat{\sigma}_{p}^2 + \frac{\hat{\sigma}_{pi}^2}{n_i} +\hat{\sigma}_{ABS}^2} \text{ .} \end{eqnarray*}\]

Which, with our data are calculated as the following: \[\begin{eqnarray*} G &=& \frac{\\3.233 + \frac{\\0.044}{\\3}} {\\3.233 + \frac{\\0.044}{\\3} + {\\0.322}} \\[5pt] &=& {\\.910}\\[20pt] \phi &=& \frac{\\3.233 + \frac{\\0.044}{\\3}} {\\3.233 + \frac{\\0.044}{\\3} + {\\0.472}} \\[5pt] &=& {\\.873 } \end{eqnarray*}\]

These estimates are a tiny bit higher than the G and phi coefficients we calculated when we treated items as a random facet (\(G\) was \(.906\) & \(phi\) was \(.847\)). If the person-item variance were very high, we would see a much larger difference between the fixed- and random-facets models’ coefficients.

11.5 Summary

G-theory provides a framework for estimating the reliability and dependability of scores when one or more facets are used in scoring persons. The formulas encompass coefficient alpha as well as the ICC estimates used in inter-rater reliability and agreement. Furthermore, the D-study methodology provides an extension of the Spearman Brown prophecy formula, permitting researchers to estimate what the reliability or dependability would be with different numbers of facet conditions. There are many possible G-theory models beyond those presented here. Two well cited resources are Brennan (2001) and Shavelson and Webb (1991).

G-theory is not the only approach for examining how well an instrument with multiple facets functions. Within modern test theory, multi-facet Rasch modeling (Engelhard, 1994; Linacre, 1989) provides methods for addressing many of these same questions and design considerations.
