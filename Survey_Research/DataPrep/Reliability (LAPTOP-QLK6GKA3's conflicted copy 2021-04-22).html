(totvar <- sum(cov(timss[,Q15])))
## [1] 17.96437

Here’s the unique variance of the items:25

(itemvarsum <- sum(apply(timss[,Q15],2,var)))
## [1] 5.224138

Here’s k, which is the number of items in the scale:

(k <- ncol(timss[,Q15]))
## [1] 7

And, here’s the alpha coefficient:

MyAlpha <- k/(k-1)*(totvar - itemvarsum)/totvar
MyAlpha
## [1] 0.8273935

6.3 Summary

There are several types of reliability coefficients. Coefficients alpha and omega are for internal consistency. Coefficient alpha is the most popular. Coefficient omega is becoming more popular because it handles some of the problems that alpha ignores.

Other coefficients, such as for estimates of stability over time, with the intra-class correlation and generalizability coefficients are addressed in measurement primers and in EDEP 616.

  1. The term covariance matrix is an abbreviation of variance-covariance matrix, which is more accurate but more cumbersome to say and write. The matrix includes the item variances on the diagonal and the covariances on the off diagonal.↩︎

  2. Note that this total variance calculated as the sum of the matrix’s cells is the same as the variance of the composite score when that score is calculated as the sum of the item scores, which is often what we do in tests—we add up each person’s points across the items to get their total score. However, to make our composite score easier to interpret on our survey scale, we have calculated each person’s score as their mean across the k items making up that scale, as this allows us to see each person’s composite score on the same scale as the meaningful points in the Likert-type scale that is used with all k of the items. For example, the first teacher’s score on the Q15 composite scale was \(2.29\), which somewhat more frequent than About half the lessons on the original Likert-type scale. That is easier to interpret than a sum score would be—it is harder to relate the person’s sum score of \(16.03\) back to a meaningful scale. Because our scale score is now divided by the number of items (\(k = 7\) in this case), this total variance differs from that of the sum of the variance-covariance cells—it is smaller because we have divided by the constant 7. By the rules of algebra of expectation, we can multiply the variance of this, which is var(timss$Q15_Index) \(= 0.37\), by this constant squared to retrieve the same variance as if the composite score were calculated as the sum across the items: var(timss$Q15_Index)*ncol(timss[,Q15])^2 \(=17.96\). This value is the same as the sum of the cells in the covariance matrix.↩︎

  3. Placing parentheses around R code forces it to be printed to the console. It’s simply a shortcut so we don’t have to type the object (totvar in this example) to view the result.↩︎

  4. Here, we’re using the double colon before the function because the function alpha() shares the same name with an argument in the ggplot package, so this psych::alpha() ensures that the function comes from the psych package.↩︎
