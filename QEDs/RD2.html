Using R for Quasi-Experimental Designs

Part 7 Regression Discontinuity, Part 2

This is Part 2 of regression discontinuity (RD) designs. This section provides an illustration of the steps for examining assumptions and some more advanced methods for estimating the effect. Most of this lesson came from a 2016 workshop in quasi-experimental designs that was provided by the Institute for Policy Research at Northwestern University and sponsored by the Institute of Education Sciences of the U.S. Department of Education. Much of the credit for the R code goes to Dr. Peter Steiner, who was an instructor at that workshop. I have adapted this code and added functions from packages. Any mistakes are due to my own misunderstandings and modifications.

The data set used in this example includes an artificial, modified, non-representative, set of data that are similar to what had been used in an RD analysis in the evaluation of five state pre-Kindergarten programs (Wong et al., 2008). The evaluation took advantage of states’ eligibility cutoffs for entry into pre-Kindergarten programs, where older children with birthdays before the state cutoff were admitted into pre-K programs for the year and children with birthdays that occurred after the state cutoff were required to wait another year for entry into pre-K. The results in my demonstration are only for learning about these decisions in RDD; they are not to be used to draw conclusions about actual Pre-K programs.

The outcomes were measured in the fall, when the treatment-group children had completed pre-K and were in kindergarten and when the comparison-group children had just started the state pre-K program. The outcomes included the Picture Peabody Vocabulary Test (PPVT), an early math test, and a print awareness skills test (PA). The data set includes several baseline demographic variables that are measures of gender, race and ethnicity, home language, and socioeconomic status.

There were issues with non-compliance: Some students who were supposed to enter pre-K were delayed a year, constituting no-shows, and some students who were supposed to wait a year entered pre-K early, constituting the crossovers. Because these are not the actual data used in the Wong, et al. (2008) study, our results will differ from what was presented in their paper.

We’ll use several packages:

  • rdd (Dimmery, 2016),
  • rddensity (Cattaneo et al., 2021),
  • rddtools (Stigler & Quast, 2021),
  • rdrobust (Calonico et al., 2021), and
  • sem (Fox et al., 2020).

7.1 Here are the steps we will take:

  1. Get to know our data by examining the treatment assignment rule and compliance
    1. Examine evidence of units’ “sorting” at the cutoff
  2. Test pre-analysis assumptions
    1. There is no sorting around the cut-off (the continuity assumption)
    2. There are no discontinuities in the covariates or pseudo-outcomes
    3. There are no unintended discontinuities in the outcome (placebo tests).
  3. Examine functional form
    1. Graphs from sharp RD parametric models
  4. Estimate sharp RD treatment effects
    1. Fitting a parametric model
    2. Fitting non-parametric models
    3. Including covariates
  5. Conduct a sensitivity analysis
  6. Account for fuzziness
    1. Fitting a parametric fuzzy RD model
    2. Fitting a non-parametric fuzzy RD model
    3. Including covariates

7.3.3 Create Bins of the Assignment Variable

When we use packages to examine RDD, we don’t need to do this part. This section is here so we can understand the process. It is also very useful, however, because including the plot of assignment variable and take-up will make our manuscript more credible, and bin sizes help to make this type of plot easier to interpret than if each assignment-variable data point (in this case, each one-day distance from the cut) had its own plot point.

Bins are what are used in histograms (and in local linear regression) and they help us to see patterns more easily; for instance, if we plotted every observation in our assignment variable (on X) and treatment take up (on Y), the line would be very jagged up-and-down, whereas with binning, we can see patterns by bin—again, this is what histograms do for us under the hood. The authors (Wong et al., 2008) used 7-day and 14-day bins because there might be fewer births on Saturdays and Sundays, so if some bins had more Saturdays and Sundays than other bins, there would be noise in the functional form that reflects this pattern (e.g., if a bin width were something like 3 days, some bins could have both weekend days whereas other bins could have neither). This type of reasoning is an example of how we use our knowledge of the context to decide on the bandwidth.39

Let’s manually create bins that are 7-day and 14-day intervals:

  • The floor() function rounds the value downwards (e.g., floor(1.4) = 1). For example, if a kid was born 27 days before the cut off and we divide by 7, and round to the floor, the result is \(\frac{27}{7} = 3.86,\) which floors to \(3\). So, everyone within 21 to 27 days will have the same floor value of 3.
  • We then give a bin value to the entire group of kids within this range by multiplying by 7 and adding half a week. So, everyone in this bin has a value of \(3 \times 7 + 3.5 = 24.5\). This bin value is the midpoint of the interval and it becomes a convenient label of the bin.

7.4 Examining Assumptions

One of the more difficult steps in carrying out quasi-experimental designs is arguing against plausible alternative explanations for the effect. This is central to the validity of our claims about causality. To help us argue against these alternative explanations, we think about assumptions and garner evidence that these assumptions have been met. There are three assumptions we can address before we conduct our main analysis:

  1. There is no sorting around the cutoff (the continuity assumption).
  2. There are no discontinuities in the covariates.
  3. There are no unintended discontinuities in the outcome (placebo tests).

7.4.1 Assumption 1—the continuity assumption

This assumption is that the potential outcomes are continuous on both sides of the cutoff. If there is manipulation of the treatment-group classification, we can no longer claim that two groups on either side of the cutoff are equal in expectation, which means that any effect we observe on the outcome in our RDD test (yet to be performed) can be conflated with unobserved covariates that are related to the manipulation.

Let’s start by plotting a histogram of the assignment variable:

par(bty = "l")

abline(v = 0, col = 'blue', lwd = 2)

Ideally, we’d want the same frequency on both sides of the cut, especially near the cut. Still, even with equal frequencies, this does not confirm that there is no manipulation because there might be wash out among covariates that explain manipulation. So, this is why it is important to also examine the discontinuities in the covariates (in a later assumption). Notice that here, there appears to be a difference between the two sides of the cut. This could be because the birth rates are this way, but it might be because parents are manipulating their kids’ reported birthdays to get them into or out of the school. Some parents might think their kids are not ready, and delay them. Others might think their kids are ready even though they’re too young, and find a way to get into the treatment. We don’t know the mechanisms of this self sorting. Some families with young kids (who would have to wait a year) might move to a different district where the cut off date permits them to enter. These undetected crossovers might then enter the study on the right-hand side of the cut.

7.4.2 Assumption 2—Discontinuities in the covariates

If this test is successful, it provides (partial) evidence that the discontinuity in our outcome (estimated subsequent to these tests) is not explained by some other mechanism. This is another way to consider manipulation and sorting. If people can sort into one or the other side of the cutpoint, we no longer have a potential outcomes framework. If sorting has occurred, it might be due to unobserved or observed covariates. The best we can do is examine the covariates we have available to identify whether there are discontinuities at our assignment variable cutoff that we use for the main analysis. If we find that any covariates or pseudo-outcomes that are expected to strongly correlate with our outcome variable are discontinuous at the cutoff, we cannot trust the estimate of the treatment’s effect on the outcome.

Our covariates are female, r_black, r_hispanic, r_white, fl_yes, hl_english, and hl_spanish. Let’s create an object that includes all of our covariate variable names.

mycovs <- c("female", "r_black", "r_hispanic", "r_white", "fl_yes", "hl_english", "hl_spanish")

Here’s a custom function that plots the scatter plot, binned data, and polynomial regression curve. Our goal is to plot the covariates by the assignment variable to see if there is a discontinuity. Let’s call our function discont().

In the code in our function, we have four arguments: y represents the covariate. It is the outcome variable in the test. av is the assignment variable. trt is the treatment indicator, binw is the bin width, main is there in case we want to add a title tot he plot; the default, with main = NULL, is to not have a title.

discont <- function(y, av, trt, binw, main = NULL){
   avbin <- floor(av/binw)*binw + binw/2  # create bin mid-points
   mbin  <- tapply(y, avbin, mean)        # covariate means of each bin (tapply is similar to the by() function)
   # fit the polynomial regression:
   out.lm <- lm(y ~ trt*(av + I(av^2) + I(av^3)))
   # plot the data :
   plot(av, y, ylab = main, cex.lab=1.5, pch = '.')  # scatterplot, specifying the x and y axis range

7.4.3 Assumption 3—Checking for pseudo-cut-offs in the outcome

What Murnane & Willett (p. 192) call pseudo-cut-offs are also sometimes called placebos. We are conducting “placebo tests” in this assumption. We can think of this as a concern about whether there are any discontinuities in the outcome variable at other regions of the assignment variable. If there are, the chance of an alternative explanation for our causal claim of interest is higher. In other words, how can we be certain that the one corresponding with our causal effect is not just another case of discontinuity that regularly occurs?

In the rddtools package, we can use the plotPlacebo() function. Notice that now our y is the outcome variable (ppvt). The rddtools package requires that we create an rdd_data output object.

7.4.3.1 Summary about RDD assumptions

In examining these assumptions, we are considering plausible alternative explanations for discontinuities that critics might bring up. Sorting or manipulation of peoples’ status on the assignment variable is tantamount to selection bias. Discontinuities in the covariates and fluctuations in discontinuities in the assignment variable can also indicate alternative explanations for the discontinuous effect that we are arguing is due to the question predictor of interest in our causal claim. With evidence that we have met these assumptions, we are making our claim about causality more convincing; we are strengthening our internal validity argument.

7.5 Examining the Parametric Functional Form

A good first step in considering functional form is viewing the plots from an over-fitting parametric (global) model. By overfitting, we mean that we fit a higher-order polynomial model. For instance, if we use a quartic model, being a fourth-order polynomial, it permits lots of bends in the relationship between the assignment variable and the outcome. \[Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_{1}\text{D}_i + \beta_{2}\text{Z}_i + \beta_{3}\text{Z}_i^2 + \beta_{4}\text{Z}_i^3 + \beta_{5}\text{Z}_i^4 + \beta_{6}\text{D}_i\text{Z}_i + \beta_{7}\text{D}_i\text{Z}_i^2 + \beta_{8}\text{D}_i\text{Z}_i^3 + \beta_{9}\text{D}_i\text{Z}_i^4 + \epsilon_i\]

In this specification of the quartic model, \(\text{D}_i\) is the treatment status of unit i \(\text{(D} = 1\) for treatment and \(\text{D} = 0\) for control, serving as a switch to activate \(\beta_6\) through \(\beta_9\) to model the slope on the right side of the cut) and \(\text{Z}_i\) is the unit’s score on the forcing variable (the continuous variable, which is days away from the cut-off date in this data set).41

The default plot in rdrobust provides this quartic-model output:

If this overfitted model showed a pattern, we could specify that in a parametric model. For instance, if the plot showed a straight line on each side instead of the wavy one we see, we might use a regular linear model to estimate the effect, such as

\[Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_{1}\text{D}_i + \beta_{2}\text{Z}_i + \beta_{3}\text{D}_i\text{Z}_i + \epsilon_i \text{.}\] If the slopes appeared to be the same on each side, and we were investigating the effect only in terms of the intercept, we could use an even simpler model, \[Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_{1}\text{D}_i + \beta_{2}\text{Z}_i + \epsilon_i \text{.}\]

If the relationship between the assignment variable and the outcome seems to change at a particular location, we might investigate substantive reasons for this based on what we know about the context and the theory. For instance, if the trend appeared to be quadratic at low or high regions of the assignment variable—away from our cut-off point—but linear in a good region around our cutoff point, we would ask why that is occurring. If we use this visual information in conjunction with our reasoning for what causes that secular trend to change at that point in the assignment variable, we can make a justification for the bandwidth of our analysis, and for our choice of parametric model within that bandwidth. With the present data, we still see some bendiness near the threshold. We might wonder, for example, why children about 150 to 180 days younger than the cut-off date seem to have lower vocabulary scores than those who are younger or older than them. We might see a different pattern with different bins. Notice, for instance, that at about 160 days younger, there is a point estimate that is higher than the regression line; if the bin width were larger, this would wash out the bend we see and we might think some kind of linear model (of the first order) would be appropriate. When we report these results, we should be sure to report which function and package we are using because the bin sizes from this function are calculated using an approach to minimize the error but also to reflect the variability in the full scatterplot42

The bias at the cut, which is what the Robust estimate adjusts for, is the incorrect estimation of the predicted intercepts of the two groups (i.e., the LATE estimate we’re interested in).

A potential problem with parametric estimations is that the prediction lines are more heavily weighted by observations away from the cut point rather than those in our LATE region. Next, let’s look at restricting the bandwidth.

7.6.2 Non-parametric Model: Local Linear Estimation

With local linear models, at least three decisions are involved in the analysis:

  1. Choosing a bandwidth (labeled as \(h\)). This can be a theory-informed decision, such as 180 days on either side of the cut, or a data-driven decision. In the rdrobust package, the default method for the data-driven selection is bwselect = "mserd", which optimizes the bandwidth given the estimated variance and bias. To allow the bandwidths on either side to differ in length, the argument bwselect = "msetwo" can be used. The rdrobust package authors document the data-driven methods in their help files and and in their accompanying monograph (Cattaneo et al., 2019).
  2. Choosing the kernel function. Using uniform kernel is tantamount to regular OLS. Using triangular or Epanechnikov kernels weight the mean of the bin more heavily, which is better than uniform unless you have no outliers in the bin.
  3. Choosing the polynomial order within the bin. The authors of this package say that linear is typically a good balance between precision and stability. However, if previous research on this subject matter used other polynomials or if the data clearly do not fit the shape, we should consider higher orders.

It appears as though we have a reasonable case to make that the pre-k experience had a causal effect on students’ vocabulary knowledge, as measured by the Picture Peabody Vocabulary Test. There are two other outcomes, the print awareness, and the Woodcock Johnson test of math reasoning. We might also wish to include covariates. If the results are consistent with these other tests, we have greater confidence that discontinuous results in one are not a fluke.

7.8 Accounting for Fuzziness

Recall from our earlier examination of the data that compliance was not 100%. About 90% of the children assigned to the enter pre-K did indeed enter pre-K. About 11% of the children assigned to the control condition (i.e., asked wait an extra year before entering pre-k), still managed to get into pre-K. The estimate we are concerned with here is the complier average treatment effect (CACE), which is also a type of LATE. In fuzzy RDDs, we have two sources of localness in estimating LATE: that due to the restricted bandwidth and that due to compliance.

If we used a two-stage-least-squares approach, allowing the slopes to differ on each side, and if we used a quadratic functional form, as the authors (Wong et al., 2008) had done, we would estimate the first stage as \[D_i = \alpha_0 + \alpha_{1}Z_i + \alpha_{2}AV_i + \alpha_{3}AV_i^2 + \alpha_{4}Z_iAV_i + \alpha_{5}Z_i AV_i^2 + \delta_i\] and the second stage as \[Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_{1}\widehat{D}_i + \beta_{2}AV_i + \beta_{3}AV_i^2 + \beta_{4}Z_iAV_i + \beta_{5}Z_iAV_i^2 + \epsilon_i \text{,}\] where \(AV\) is the continuous assignment variable (AKA forcing variable, which is avar in our data), \(Z\) is the categorical treatment-assignment variable (treatment in our data), and \(D\) is the treatment-received variable (takeup in our data).

Notice that the bandwidth, and the effective number of observations, are lower than the model in which we did not account for fuzziness. Compared to the sharp RDD model, we see a larger estimated effect but a higher standard error, which makes sense because we are accounting for cross-overs and never-takers, while also reducing the size of our analytic sample. This reduced sample size hurt the feelings of our desperate researcher hoping to find an effect—that warm and fuzzy feeling is lost. We have a smaller test statistic and a confidence interval that includes zero (even with the less conservative conventional method). In other words, when we account for the fuzziness with these data, the causal effect is not significant.

7.8.2 Summary about fuzzy RDD

We saw some differences in the fuzzy and sharp RDD results. Our interpretation of the estimated causal effect in fuzzy designs is restricted to compliers. Whereas the sharp RDD is useful for research questions about the intent to treat, which may be valuable for drawing conclusions about the value of a program as it is provided in the wild, fuzzy RDD is useful for research questions about the effects of the treatment itself.

One final word. These results are from an artificial, non-representative, sample of data. The results in this demonstration are only for learning about these decisions in RDD; they are not to be used to draw conclusions about actual Pre-K programs.
